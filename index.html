A little studio know-how

Use the numbers. Trust your ears.

What does pre-delay do?

Pre-delay is the gap between the dry sound and the start of the reverb. A little space can help keep the original attack clear. Try a short subdivision, then adjust it while listening to the whole mix.

Why follow the tempo?

Musical subdivisions give you repeatable starting points. A quarter-note delay repeats once per beat; an eighth-note delay repeats twice. Dotted and triplet settings give different rhythmic patterns.

Timing a reverb tail

The custom calculator subtracts pre-delay from your chosen total length. This is a timing convention, not a promise that the tail stops on that beat. Many decay controls represent RT60: the time taken for the reverberant level to fall by 60 dB.

Making room in a mix

For a snare on beats 2 and 4 in 4/4, two beats is a useful place to start when shaping the tail before the next hit. Shorten it if the groove gets blurred. Filtering the reverb return or ducking it beneath the dry sound can also improve clarity.

How the maths works

Quarter note (ms) = 60,000 ÷ BPM. A whole note lasts four quarter notes. Dotted values multiply by 1.5; triplets multiply by 2/3. Frequency (Hz) = 1,000 ÷ time in milliseconds.

At 120 BPM, two beats last 1,000 ms. A 1/64-note pre-delay is 31.25 ms, leaving a target decay of 968.75 ms.

Beats here are quarter notes. A whole note is one bar only in 4/4; the beats-per-bar control changes bar lengths.
